Tonight at the Tower — Privacy Policy

Last updated: October 16, 2026

Tonight at the Tower is a free game for Android phones, and later for iPhone and iPad. It is published by Cardwell Software LLC, a company in the United States. This page says what the game sends from your phone, why, who receives it, how long it is kept, and what you can do about it. On this page "Europe" means the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom and Switzerland.

The short version

  • Before you can play, the game's first page asks you to agree to the terms of use and to this policy. Nothing is sent before you agree.
  • There is no account and no sign-in. The game never asks for your name, email, contacts or precise location.
  • In Europe the game sends no statistics at all, and it shows ads only to players who say they are 16 or over.
  • Your progress and settings are kept on your phone. Your phone's own backup, if you have it on, can carry them to a new phone.
  • Basic statistics, outside Europe: a small daily count with no identifier in it. On Android it is on unless you switch it off, from the moment you agree on the first page. On an iPhone it is off until you say yes when the game asks, after your first run.
  • Run data, outside Europe: which cards you pick and how your fights and runs go. It is sent only if you say yes when the game asks, after your first run. On an iPhone that one question asks about both kinds of statistics.
  • Ads: after your first five runs the game shows ads from Google. Google's ad software uses your phone's advertising ID; on an iPhone, only if you allow it when the iPhone asks. In Europe the game first asks whether you are 16 or over, and Google's form then asks what you allow.
  • One thing is for sale, "Remove ads". The store handles the payment and we never see your payment details.
  • You can change every one of these choices in the game's Settings, and you can ask to see, copy or delete what is stored about you.

Who we are

Cardwell Software LLC publishes the game and decides what it collects and why. In the words of data protection law, it is the "controller" of that data. It is a company in the United States.

Write to us about anything on this page at cardwell.software.llc@gmail.com. In the game, Settings has a "Contact us" plate that opens an email to the same address.

In Europe: no statistics, and ads only at 16 or over

On a phone that the game takes to be in Europe:

  • the game sends no basic statistics and no run data. It does not ask about either, and Settings has no switch for them, because there is nothing to switch
  • before the first ad would appear, the game asks whether you are 16 or over. If you say you are under 16, you get no ads at all. If you say 16 or over, Google's consent form asks what you allow. Ads has the detail
  • the rest of this page is the same as anywhere else: what stays on your phone, the purchase, and the emails you send us

How the game decides. It looks, on the phone, at the phone's region setting and at its time zone. If either one says Europe, or if the game cannot tell, it treats the phone as in Europe. Neither is sent anywhere or stored.

The game goes by the phone's settings, not by where you are standing. A phone set to a region outside Europe, and running on a clock from outside Europe, is treated as outside Europe wherever it is.

What stays on your phone

Your settings and progress, a run in progress, the records of up to a hundred past runs, the choices you have made about the things on this page, a note that you agreed on the game's first page (to which version of the terms and of this policy, and when), your answer to the age question if the game asked it, and a note of what you have bought are kept in the game's own storage on your phone. The game does not send them to us.

Your phone's backup. If you have your phone's own backup on, your settings, your progress, your run in progress, your past runs and your answer to the age question are included in it, so a new phone or a fresh install can carry on where you left off. The random install ID used for run data, the note that you agreed on the first page, and anything still waiting to be sent, are not included: a restored phone asks you to agree again. The backup is Google's service on Android and Apple's on an iPhone (iCloud Backup, or a backup to a computer), kept under your own account with them. We never receive it. You switch it on or off in your phone's own Settings.

Clearing the game's storage, or uninstalling the game, removes all of this from the phone. It does not remove it from a backup that you keep.

Basic statistics: a daily count with no identifier (not in Europe)

In Europe the game sends no basic statistics. Outside Europe, at most once on a day when you open the game, it sends one count. The count holds:

  • a random value made for that one count and used for nothing else. It is never stored on your phone and never repeated, so it does not join one day's count to another's
  • the day, as your phone's calendar has it, with no time of day
  • the game's version, and the version of its content
  • whether the phone runs Android or iOS, and the system's main version number, such as 14
  • the day of this phone's first count, and the number of days since then
  • the minutes played since the count before, in whole minutes and never more than 720
  • whether the game is a development build

Nothing else is in it: no install ID, no advertising ID, no phone model, no account, no name, no location, no language and no country.

That is a count with no identifier. It is not a promise that a phone's counts could never be told apart. The day of the first count, the system and the game's version are the same on every count a phone sends, so in a very small group of players one phone's counts could be recognised as coming from the same phone.

  • On or off. Outside Europe, on an Android phone, basic statistics are on unless you switch them off. The game's first page asks you to agree to the terms of use and to this policy before you play, and nothing is counted or sent before you agree. On an iPhone they are off until you say yes: when your first run ends, the game asks once whether you want to share statistics, and one yes switches on both basic statistics and run data. Until then nothing is counted or sent. On both, the switch is in the game's Settings, and your own setting is remembered. The game plays the same either way. In Europe the first page is the same page, but agreeing there switches no statistics on: there is no switch, and nothing is counted.
  • Kept on your phone for it. The day of the first count, the last day counted, play time not yet sent, your on or off choice, and a count that has been made but not yet delivered. Up to thirty days' counts wait for a connection; older ones are dropped. Switching off drops the play time and the counts that were waiting.
  • Why. To see how many people play, for how long, whether they come back, and which versions of the game and of the phone's system are in use.
  • Legal basis. On Android outside Europe, where they are on unless you switch them off: our legitimate interest in knowing whether the game is played and on which systems. On an iPhone: your consent, given when you say yes. In Europe they are not collected, so there is no basis to give.
  • Who receives it. PostHog, the analytics service that runs on our behalf. See PostHog's privacy policy.
  • Where. On PostHog's servers in Frankfurt, Germany. We read the totals from the United States.
  • How long. One year. After that it is no longer available to us.

Run data: your choice, off until you say yes (not in Europe)

In Europe the game never asks about run data and never sends it.

Outside Europe, when your first run ends, the game asks once whether you want to share run data. Until you say yes, none is sent. Sharing is not needed to play: if you say no, nothing in the game is held back. You can change your answer at any time in the game's Settings.

On an iPhone the same question asks about basic statistics too, with one yes or no for both. After that each has its own switch in the game's Settings.

If you say yes, the game sends:

  • what happens when you play: the cards you are offered and the ones you pick, your fights and how they go, the floors you reach, what you choose in shops and events, how each run ends, and when your sessions start and stop
  • how the controls feel: how you cast your cards, the time spent dragging, cancelled drags and missed taps. Never a recording of your screen. No message is sent for each card you play: a run's totals go with its end
  • a few of the game's own settings, such as which hand you play with
  • device basics: phone model, system version, screen shape and the game's version
  • error reports when something in the game breaks: a short fingerprint of the error and the place in the game's code where it happened, not the error's text
  • what happens with ads and the purchase: that an ad was shown, closed, skipped or failed to load, and how long it was shown; and that "Remove ads" was started, bought, refunded or restored, with its price and its currency. Never the order's number, and nothing about how you paid
  • a random install ID, made at the moment you say yes, that groups your runs together, and the number of days since then. It is not your advertising ID, and the game never joins it to your name or to an account
  • Why. To see which cards, fights and floors need fixing, where the controls or the game itself go wrong, and whether the ads and the purchase work as they should. It is not used to choose the ads you see, and we do not sell it.
  • Legal basis. Your consent. In Europe it is not collected, so there is no basis to give.
  • Who receives it. PostHog, on our behalf. We give it to nobody else.
  • Where. On PostHog's servers in Frankfurt, Germany. We read it from the United States.
  • How long. One year, or until you have it deleted, whichever comes first.
  • If you switch it off. The game sends one last notice that you have opted out, together with anything that was still waiting to be sent. Then it stops and forgets the install ID. What was sent before stays for the rest of its year, unless you have it deleted. Your choices and rights says how.

Your internet address, and what every message carries

Anything sent over the internet arrives from an internet (IP) address, and the statistics above are no different. PostHog sees that address when a message arrives. The game tells PostHog not to work out a location from it, and we have set PostHog to discard the address instead of storing it. PostHog records the time each message arrived.

Each message also carries the ordinary technical headers of a web request. They name the game's engine and the phone's system (Android or iOS). There is no cookie.

Ads

The game shows full-screen ads through Google AdMob. There are none during your first five runs. After that an ad may appear at a break in play: when a boss is beaten, and when a run ends. Never in the middle of a fight.

In Europe, one question first. Before the first ad would appear, the game asks: "Are you 16 or over?"

  • "Under 16". You get no ads at all. Google's ad software is never started on your phone, so nothing in this section is collected, and "Remove ads" is not offered, because there are no ads to remove.
  • "16 or over". Google's consent form follows, and then ads as anywhere else.

The game takes your word and asks for no proof. Either answer lets you play the whole game. Your answer stays on your phone, and in your phone's own backup if you have that on. It is never sent to us or to Google. You can give it again in the game's Settings, under "Age for ads", and an answer of "Under 16" holds wherever the phone is used afterwards. Outside Europe no age is asked.

To choose and show ads, Google's ad software inside the game collects these things and sends them to Google:

  • your phone's advertising ID, a resettable ID that the phone provides for advertising (on an iPhone, only if you allow it: see Apple's tracking question, below); an ID that the phone gives to apps from one developer, such as Android's app set ID; and, where it applies, other identifiers related to accounts signed in on the phone
  • your internet (IP) address, which Google may use to estimate your general location
  • how you use the app and its ads: that the app was opened, taps on an ad, whether a video ad was watched
  • diagnostics about how the app and the ad software perform

We decide that the game shows Google's ads. Google decides what its software collects and how that is used, under its own privacy policy. We do not receive your advertising ID. What Google reports to us is totals: how many ads were shown and what they earned.

Google's consent form. When the ad software starts, the first thing it does is ask Google whether a consent form is due where you are. In Europe that happens only after you have said you are 16 or over. Where a form is due, it appears before any ad is requested. It names the advertising companies Google works with and asks what you allow. Your answer decides what the ad software is allowed to store, read and use, and whether ads are personalised. You can say no and still play. "Ad privacy choices" in the game's Settings opens the form again, so you can change or take back your answer whenever you like.

On an iPhone, Apple's tracking question. It comes with Google's consent form, the first time an ad would show: never at launch and never in your first five runs, and in Europe only after you have said you are 16 or over. A short page from Google says what comes next. Then the iPhone itself asks whether the game may track you, with our reason: "Lets the ads you see be chosen for you. The game plays the same whatever you choose." If you allow it, Google's ad software may read the iPhone's advertising ID. If you ask the game not to track, it cannot read it, the ads are chosen without it, and the game plays the same. The iPhone asks only once. You can change your answer in the iPhone's Settings, under Privacy & Security, then Tracking.

Everywhere. You can reset or delete your advertising ID, or turn ad personalisation off, in your phone's own Settings: on most Android phones under Google, then Ads; on an iPhone under Privacy & Security, then Tracking. Google describes Android's on its Advertising ID help page.

If you own "Remove ads". The game does not start Google's ad software, so no ads are shown and nothing in this section is collected through the game.

  • Why. The game is free, and the ads pay for it.
  • Legal basis. In Europe: your consent, given in Google's form after you have said you are 16 or over. From a player who says they are under 16, nothing is collected. Elsewhere: our legitimate interest in paying for a free game with advertising, and your consent where Google's form asks for it.
  • Who receives it. Google, and the advertising companies Google works with to deliver and measure an ad. See Google's privacy policy and How Google uses information from sites or apps that use our services.
  • Where. On Google's servers, in the United States and other countries.
  • How long. Google decides, under its own policy. We hold none of it.

The purchase

"Remove ads" is bought once, through Google Play (on an iPhone, through Apple's App Store), with the payment method in your account there. The store handles the payment. It tells the game what you own, with the order's number and the time it was bought, and nothing about how you paid. The game keeps a note that you own it on your phone, so that it also works without a connection.

We never see your card number or any other payment details. The store keeps the record of the sale and shows us the orders for the game, which is how refunds are handled.

In Europe, a player who has said they are under 16 is not offered "Remove ads". They are shown no ads, so there is nothing to remove.

  • Why, and the legal basis. It is needed for the contract: to give you what you bought, and to deal with a refund.
  • Who receives it. The store you bought it from: Google, or Apple.
  • How long. The store keeps its record of the sale under its own policy. The note on your phone stays until you clear the game's storage.
  • If you buy nothing. None of this happens. The game is played in full without it.

Emails you send us

The "Contact us" plate in the game's Settings opens your own email app on a letter to us. The game itself sends nothing: you send the letter, from your own address. It starts with two lines that you can see and delete. One gives the game's version and the version of its content. The other gives your phone's model and its system version.

When you write to us, by that plate or any other way, we receive your email address and the name your email app sends with it, what you wrote, and those two lines if you left them in.

  • Why. To answer you, and to fix the faults you report.
  • Legal basis. Our legitimate interest in answering the people who write to us.
  • Who receives it. Our mailbox is a Gmail mailbox, so Google stores the emails for us.
  • Where. In that mailbox, on Google's servers. We read it in the United States.
  • How long. For as long as we need the email to answer you and to fix what you reported. Ask, and we will delete it sooner.

The store's own statistics

Apart from anything the game does, Google Play tells every developer how its app is doing: how many people install it, open it, keep it or uninstall it, and crash reports from phones whose owners share diagnostics with Google. Google collects these under its own terms, and what it shows us does not name anyone. The game contains no code for this.

Apple's App Store does the same for the iPhone game: how many people see it in the store, download it and buy in it, and, from iPhones whose owners chose in Settings to share analytics with app developers, how often it is opened, how long it is kept, and crash reports. Apple collects these under its own terms, and what it shows us does not name anyone. The game contains no code for this either.

The game's permissions

On Android, the store lists what an app is allowed to do on a phone. This is the game's list, and what each thing is for:

  • use the internet: for what this page says the game sends, for ads and for the store
  • vibrate the phone: the game's own touches of vibration
  • read the advertising ID, and use Google Play's billing: for the ads and for the purchase
  • see whether the phone is online, read basic facts about the phone's state such as the kind of mobile network it is on, keep the phone awake while a task finishes, and run a task in the foreground: these four come with Google's ad software and the libraries it is built on

On an iPhone, the game asks for one permission: Apple's tracking question, for the ads, as Ads says. It does not ask to send you notifications.

On neither does the game have permission for your location, your contacts, your photos or files, your camera or your microphone.

What we never collect

The game does not ask for, read or send:

  • your name, email address, phone number or contacts. We learn your email address only if you write to us
  • an account or a password: the game has no sign-in
  • your age or your date of birth. In Europe the game asks whether you are 16 or over, and that answer is never sent
  • your precise location
  • your photos, files, camera or microphone
  • anything from your other apps

Google's ad software works under Google's own rules. What it collects is listed under Ads.

Where your data is handled

  • Germany. PostHog stores the basic statistics and the run data on its servers in Frankfurt. PostHog, Inc. is a company in the United States. From a phone the game takes to be in Europe, nothing goes to PostHog but one kind of message: a request to delete what was sent before, or a notice that run data was switched off, where you made one and it had not yet been sent.
  • The United States. Cardwell Software LLC is there. We read the statistics from there, and the emails you send us arrive there.
  • The United States and other countries. Google handles what its ad software and its store collect on its own servers.

The European Union and the United Kingdom have made no general decision that the United States protects personal data as their own law does. Google LLC is certified under the EU–US Data Privacy Framework, the UK extension to it and the Swiss–US Data Privacy Framework, which were set up for that purpose, and it uses standard contractual clauses where those do not apply. Google sets this out on its page about data transfer frameworks. What we handle ourselves, we handle as this page says, wherever you live.

Your choices and rights

The law of the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom and Switzerland gives people there rights over data about them. We give the same to every player. Using them costs nothing, and we answer within one month.

In Europe. The game sends no statistics from a phone it takes to be in Europe. It makes no install ID there, and we hold nothing from the game that is yours to show, copy or delete. What can exist is held by others or came from you directly: ads data with Google, if you said you are 16 or over and agreed in Google's form; a purchase with the store; and any emails you sent us. Each has its paragraph below. If you agreed to share run data somewhere else and then open the game in Europe, the game switches run data off, discards anything waiting to be sent and sends nothing. It keeps your install ID on the phone only so that "Delete my data" in Settings still works there; that request is the one thing the game sends from there. Run data stays off when you leave again, until you switch it on in Settings. What was sent before is kept until it is a year old unless you have it deleted.

Stop it. Any yes you gave to statistics or to ads, you can take back as easily. Outside Europe, basic statistics and run data each have a switch in the game's Settings. "Ad privacy choices" there reopens Google's ad form. In Europe, "Age for ads" lets you answer the age question again. On an iPhone, Apple's tracking question is answered again in the iPhone's Settings, under Privacy & Security, then Tracking. The backup is switched off in your phone's own Settings. Stopping does not undo what was sent before, and nothing in the game is taken away.

Object. Where basic statistics are on unless you switch them off, you can object to them at any time and without giving a reason: switch them off in the game's Settings. Nothing more is sent.

See it, or get a copy. While run data is on, "Delete my data" in the game's Settings shows your install ID, with a button that copies it. Send us that ID by email and ask for a copy. We send you what is stored under it, in a file a computer can read.

Delete it. On that same paper, "Delete what was sent" asks us to delete everything stored under your install ID. Anything still waiting on your phone is discarded, not sent: only the request and a notice that run data is off leave the phone. The game then switches run data off and forgets the ID. We delete what was stored within one month.

After run data has been switched off. The game no longer knows your install ID, and nothing we hold says which records came from your phone. Unless you kept the ID and send it to us, we cannot find those records to copy or delete them. When they are a year old they are no longer available to us.

Basic statistics. They carry no identifier, so we have no way to look up the counts that came from your phone, and cannot copy or delete them for you. Switch them off to stop them.

Correct it, or have its use paused. If something we hold about you is wrong, tell us and we will correct it. You can also ask us to keep your data without using it while a question about it is settled.

Ads. Google holds that data, not us. Resetting or deleting your advertising ID in your phone's Settings cuts the tie to your phone, and Google's privacy policy says how to ask Google to show you or delete what it holds.

Emails. Write to us, and we will send you a copy of what we hold from you or delete it.

On your phone. Clear the game's storage in your phone's Settings, or uninstall the game. A backup that you keep is deleted in your Google or Apple account.

To find run data we need your install ID. We ask for nothing else to prove who you are.

Complaints

If you think we have handled your data wrongly, please tell us at cardwell.software.llc@gmail.com. We confirm that we have your complaint within 30 days, look into it, and tell you what we found.

You also have the right to complain to a data protection authority, at any time:

  • United Kingdom: the ICO, through its page Make a complaint about how an organisation has used your personal information.
  • European Economic Area: the authority of the country where you live or work, or where the problem happened. The European Data Protection Board lists them all.
  • Switzerland: the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner.

Automated decisions

Nothing in the game decides anything about you by automated means that has a legal effect on you or affects you in a similarly serious way. Google's ad software chooses which ad to show. Where personalised ads are on, it chooses from what Google knows of your interests.

Ages

Tonight at the Tower is for players aged 13 and over. It is not made for younger children, and we do not knowingly collect personal information from anyone under 13.

In Europe. The game sends no statistics for a player of any age. Ads are only for players who say they are 16 or over. The game asks before the first ad, and a player who answers "Under 16" is shown none and has no ad software started on their phone. The game takes the answer on trust: it does not check it, and it never sends it.

Outside Europe. No age is asked. If the law where you live says that someone your age needs a parent's or guardian's agreement before their data is used, ask them before you say yes to sharing statistics.

A parent or guardian who believes a child has shared data through the game can write to us, and we will help to have it deleted in the way set out above.

How it is kept safe

Everything the game sends travels over an encrypted connection. The statistics are held by PostHog, and ad and purchase data by Google, each under its own security. The game stores no password, because there is nothing to sign in to.
